We’ll learn how to solve this question:

**In the Bohr model, which two have equal orbit radii: H in its first orbit, He⁺ in its first or second orbit, Li²⁺ in its first orbit, or Be³⁺ in its second orbit?**

Adapted from [JEE Main, 4 April 2026 Shift 2 Q52](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s3f8e59f4b2fe7c5705bf878bbd494ccdf/uploads/2026/04/20260409432593766.pdf). We’ll compare the allowed orbit radii using the orbit number and nuclear charge. You need squares and ratios.

## One electron, different nuclei

Hydrogen has one electron. So do He⁺, Li²⁺ and Be³⁺: each ion has lost all but one of its electrons. Their nuclei have different numbers of protons. We write that number as Z: it is 1 for H, 2 for He, 3 for Li and 4 for Be.

The Bohr model describes the electron using allowed circular orbits. Label an orbit by the positive whole number n: n = 1 is the first orbit and n = 2 is the second. This is a useful model for these exam calculations, though the modern quantum description does not treat an electron as travelling on a fixed circular track.

For a one-electron species, the Bohr orbit radius is:

**r = a₀n²/Z.**

Here a₀ is the radius of the first Bohr orbit of hydrogen, about 52.9 pm. A picometre (pm) is 10⁻¹² m. [OpenStax derives this relation](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-2-the-bohr-model) and discusses the model’s limits.

<ArticleDiagram kind="bohrRadius">The circles represent Bohr-model orbit radii, not observed electron paths. Hydrogen at n = 1 has radius a₀. For Be³⁺ at n = 2, n²/Z is 4/4, so its radius is also a₀.</ArticleDiagram>

## Compare the factors

The constant a₀ is the same in each calculation. To compare radii, calculate n²/Z for each species. If those factors match, the radii match in this model.

For H in the first orbit, n²/Z = 1²/1 = 1. For Be³⁺ in the second orbit, n²/Z = 2²/4 = 1. Both radii are a₀. The larger orbit number increases the radius by a factor of four, while the larger nuclear charge divides it by four.

Be careful to square n before dividing by Z. A second orbit does not simply double the first orbit’s radius for the same nucleus: it has four times the radius.

<KeyIdea id="bohr-factor">For a one-electron species in the Bohr model, compare n²/Z, not n/Z.</KeyIdea>

## Try the past questions

**1. A one-electron species has a Bohr orbit radius of 70.53 pm. Using a₀ = 52.9 pm, which listed choice fits: Li²⁺ at n = 3, He⁺ at n = 3, He⁺ at n = 2, or Li²⁺ at n = 2?**

Adapted from [JEE Main, 6 April 2026, Shift 2, Q52](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s3f8e59f4b2fe7c5705bf878bbd494ccdf/uploads/2026/04/20260409725707538.pdf).

<details class="reading-check"><summary>Show hint</summary><p>Calculate n²/Z for each listed choice, then multiply by 52.9 pm.</p></details>

<details class="reading-check"><summary>Show solution</summary><p>For Li²⁺ at n = 2, Z = 3 and n²/Z = 4/3. Its radius is 52.9 × 4/3 = 70.53 pm to two decimal places.</p><p>The other choices give 158.7 pm, 238.05 pm and 105.8 pm, respectively. The matching choice is <strong>Li²⁺ at n = 2</strong>.</p></details>

**2. Return to our opening question: which two of the five listed orbits have equal radii?**

<details class="reading-check"><summary>Show hint</summary><p>In the listed order, calculate n²/Z for H, He⁺, He⁺, Li²⁺ and Be³⁺.</p></details>

<details class="reading-check"><summary>Show solution</summary><p>The factors are 1, 1/2, 2, 1/3 and 1. The first and fifth match.</p><p>So <strong>H in its first orbit and Be³⁺ in its second orbit</strong> have equal radii in the Bohr model.</p></details>
