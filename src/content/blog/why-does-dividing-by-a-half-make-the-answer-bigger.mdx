Division often seems to make an answer smaller. So why does 2 ÷ ½ give 4?

Imagine a ribbon that is 2 metres long. You want pieces that are half a metre long. We’ll count those pieces to see what this division is asking us to find.

## Count the half-metre pieces

A <ReadingTerm term="Half" definition="One of two equal parts of a whole. Half a metre is 0.5 metres, or 50 centimetres.">half</ReadingTerm> is one of two equal parts. So the first metre of ribbon gives you two half-metre pieces. The second metre gives you another two.

That makes **four pieces altogether**.

We can write this as **2 ÷ ½ = 4**. Read it as: “How many halves fit into two?”

Here, <ReadingTerm term="Division" definition="When the size of each group is known, division tells us how many such groups fit into the total. Here, the groups are half-metre pieces.">division</ReadingTerm> is counting pieces of a known size. The answer, 4, means four pieces. The ribbon is still only 2 metres long.

With 1-metre pieces, there are two. With half-metre pieces, there are four. The full length of ribbon stays the same.

## Smaller pieces, more pieces

The bigger answer comes from counting smaller pieces. Each metre contains two halves, so there are twice as many half-metres as metres.

<KeyIdea id="halves-key-idea">Dividing by ½ counts halves. Each whole contains two halves, so the count doubles.</KeyIdea>

You may have learnt the shortcut “divide by ½, multiply by 2”. The ribbon shows why it works: **there are two halves to count in every whole**.

## “Divide by two” asks a different question

Suppose you want to share the same 2-metre ribbon into **two equal pieces**. Each piece will be 1 metre long: 2 ÷ 2 = 1.

Compare the two requests carefully. “Make two equal pieces” tells you how many pieces you want. “Make half-metre pieces” tells you how long each piece should be.

For this ribbon, the first request gives two 1-metre pieces. The second gives four half-metre pieces. Saying what you are counting makes the calculation much easier to understand.
