If every push has an equal push back, why doesn’t everything stay still? Let’s use one ordinary step to find out. We’ll look at what pushes you forwards and where the opposite push goes.

In physics, a <ReadingTerm term="Force" definition="A push or a pull on an object. A force can change how the object moves.">force</ReadingTerm> is simply a push or a pull on an object.

## Start with a step

Think about the moment you push off to walk forwards. Your shoe presses **backwards against the ground**. The ground grips your shoe and pushes it **forwards**. This forward push helps your body move.

These two pushes happen together. They are equally strong and point in opposite directions.

<WalkingForces />

Point to the shoe: the blue arrow shows the push on it. Now point to the ground: the orange arrow shows the push on the ground. Each arrow belongs to a different object.

## Why don’t they cancel?

Look at where each push acts. The ground’s forward push acts on your foot, and therefore on you. Your backward push acts on the ground.

The backward push is not a backward force on you. So it cannot cancel the forward force on you. Each of these two forces acts on a different object.

<KeyIdea id="forces-key-idea">These equal and opposite forces act on different objects. This is Newton’s third law.</KeyIdea>

## The ground needs to grip your shoe

That grip is called <ReadingTerm term="Friction" definition="A force between touching surfaces that resists sliding. Here, it keeps your shoe from slipping backwards.">friction</ReadingTerm>. It lets you push against the ground without your shoe sliding backwards.

This is why walking on smooth ice is difficult. Your shoe has much less grip, so it is harder to get the forward push you need.

When you hear “equal and opposite”, ask one more question: **which object does each force act on?**
