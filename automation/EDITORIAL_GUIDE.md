# Padho.ai technical blog editorial guide

## Editorial promise

Each post explains one important idea about learning technology.

The idea must be technically serious. The writing must stay simple. A reader
should be able to repeat the main point after one read.

Write for a curious parent or teacher first. Engineers and researchers should
still find the idea useful, but they are not allowed to set the language. It is
not a stream of product announcements. It is not an SEO farm.

Sound human. Plain writing is not a simplified version that comes after the
technical version. It is the version we publish.

## Required shape

- Make one clear claim. State it near the start.
- Explain one mechanism. Show how it works.
- Use one concrete example.
- Include one limitation, failure case, or counterargument.
- End with a useful implication. Do not end with a sales pitch.
- Aim for 900 to 1,400 words. Never exceed 1,600 words.
- Use three to six `##` sections.
- Use at least four sources from at least three domains.
- Prefer primary research, official engineering posts, standards, and public
  technical documentation.

## Distinctness rules

- Compare the idea and execution with the current Padho blog library.
- A new title on an old argument is not a new post.
- Do not reuse the same classroom subject or worked example in nearby posts.
- Do not default to fractions when any clear example would work.
- Vary the opening device. A post can start with a result, system failure,
  design constraint, field observation, or surprising comparison.
- Vary the narrative shape. Rotate between a technical teardown, evidence
  review, metric analysis, architecture explanation, and implementation note.
- Do not repeat the "student is stuck, tutor helps, student succeeds" arc.
- Before submission, identify the closest existing post. Explain how the new
  draft differs in its claim, example, and structure.

## Clarity rules

- Deep tech means a useful mechanism explained clearly. It does not mean dense
  terminology.
- A thoughtful parent or teacher should understand the main point from the
  title and first five sentences.
- Prefer a title that a parent or teacher might say aloud. Do not invent a
  clever label for the idea.
- State the idea in everyday language before naming a technical term.
- Use a technical term only when the reader needs it to understand the idea.
  If ordinary words carry the same meaning, use the ordinary words.
- Use no more than three necessary specialist terms in the whole article,
  excluding source titles. Define each one beside a concrete example.
- Do not put an acronym, formula, model name, or research-paper phrase in the
  title or opening.
- Use a formula only when the article would become misleading without it. A
  formula must never be the main way the reader learns the mechanism.
- Explain what one person sees, does, or decides before describing a system,
  model, metric, or abstraction.
- Never make the reader decode a title such as "the experiment unit" or a
  phrase such as "cluster contamination."
- Short sentences can still be abstract. Prefer people, actions, and visible
  outcomes over strings of concepts.
- Do not sound like a research abstract, conference paper, or consultant deck.
- Do not arrange the article as a tour of studies, tools, or model names.
  Research should support the story after the idea is already clear.
- If the whole article cannot be explained in one plain sentence, reject it.

## Sentence and paragraph rules

- Prefer sentences between 8 and 18 words.
- The average sentence must stay below 20 words.
- No sentence may exceed 34 words.
- Use at most two commas in a sentence. Split the sentence instead.
- Do not use semicolons.
- Use no more than two em dashes in the whole article.
- Keep paragraphs to one, two, or three sentences.
- Start with the point. Do not warm up for three paragraphs.
- Use common words and concrete verbs.
- Define technical terms in plain language on first use.

## Evidence rules

- Put a Markdown link next to the claim it supports.
- Include a `## Sources` section with four to eight sources.
- Never invent a study, statistic, quotation, student story, or Padho metric.
- Never imply that correlation proves learning.
- Say when evidence is early, small, contested, or indirect.
- Do not copy a source's structure or wording. Build a new argument from facts.
- Do not quote more than one short phrase from any source.

## Voice

- Calm, direct, curious, and precise.
- Sound like a thoughtful person explaining something useful at a kitchen
  table. Do not perform intelligence or try to sound impressive.
- Use `we` only for work Padho has actually done.
- Mention Padho at most twice, and only when the connection is real.
- Prefer a small useful truth over a grand prediction.

## Banned habits

Do not use these phrases or close variants:

- in today's fast-paced world
- ever-evolving landscape
- delve into
- game-changer
- revolutionize
- unlock the power
- harness the power
- seamless or seamlessly
- robust solution
- transformative journey
- it is important to note
- at its core
- moreover
- furthermore
- in conclusion
- the future is here
- paradigm shift
- tapestry
- realm

Avoid repeated contrast formulas such as `not X, but Y`. One can be effective.
Five in one post sounds generated.

Avoid adjective stacks. Avoid three-part lists when two concrete points are
enough. Avoid rhetorical questions unless the question drives the whole post.

## Final test

Before submitting, answer these questions:

1. What is the one idea?
2. What evidence makes it credible?
3. What does the reader understand now that they did not understand before?
4. Which sentence can be removed without losing meaning?
5. Does any sentence sound like it could appear on any AI company's blog?
6. Can a reader explain the point without using a technical term?
7. Which words would a parent or teacher have to look up?
8. Does the article still work after removing every model name and acronym?

Remove every sentence that fails the fifth test.
If more than three specialist terms survive the seventh test, rewrite the
article before submitting it.
