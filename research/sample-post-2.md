Your child finishes their maths homework in 20 minutes. All answers correct. You feel relieved.

Two weeks later, the same topic appears on a test. They score 4 out of 10.

What happened between the homework and the test is the most important thing about your child's education — and almost no parent sees it. The homework was memory. The test required understanding. They are not the same thing.

You cannot watch your child's brain while they study. But you can ask three questions that reveal, in under a minute, whether they memorised the steps or understood the concept.

---

## Question 1: "Can you explain *why* this step works?"

Not "what is the answer?" Not "what is the next step?" *Why does this step work?*

Try it with any problem your child just solved. Pick a step from the middle — not the first step (too easy to parrot) and not the last step (they might just remember the answer).

**What memorisation sounds like:** "Because that is the rule." "Because ma'am told us to do it this way." "Because you cross-multiply."

**What understanding sounds like:** "Because you cannot add fractions with different denominators — it is like adding apples and oranges. So you need to find a common denominator first." They might not use these exact words. They might stumble. That is fine. You are listening for *reasoning*, not polish.

If they cannot explain why a step works, they do not understand it. They copied a procedure. And that procedure will evaporate by the time the exam arrives.

---

## Question 2: "What would happen if I changed this number?"

Take any problem they just solved and change one value. Not the whole problem — just one number.

If they solved 3/4 + 2/3, ask: "What if it were 3/4 + 2/5 instead? Would the method change?"

**What memorisation sounds like:** Panic. "Wait, let me start over." They treat each problem as a brand-new task because they only memorised this specific arrangement.

**What understanding sounds like:** "The method is the same — I still need a common denominator. It just changes from 12 to 20." They see the *structure*, not just the specific numbers. They can adapt.

This question is particularly powerful for algebra and geometry, where a small change in the given values should not change the approach. If your child treats every variation as a new problem, they are operating from memory, not understanding.

---

## Question 3: "Can you teach this to me?"

This is the most revealing question of all.

Ask your child to explain the concept to you as if you have never seen it before. Not the problem — the *concept*. "Teach me what a common denominator is." "Teach me why we factorise quadratics."

**What memorisation sounds like:** They repeat the textbook definition verbatim. "A common denominator is the least common multiple of the denominators of two or more fractions." Correct — but they are reciting, not teaching.

**What understanding sounds like:** "Okay, so imagine you have two different-sized pieces of a roti. One is cut into four parts and one is cut into three parts. You cannot compare them directly because the pieces are different sizes. So you re-cut both into the same size — that is the common denominator." They reach for an analogy. They use their own words. They try to make *you* understand.

Teaching is the highest form of understanding. A student who can teach a concept has not just memorised it — they have internalised it deeply enough to reconstruct it from scratch.

---

## When to ask these questions

**Not during homework.** Your child is already under pressure. These questions during homework feel like an interrogation.

**After homework, casually.** Over dinner, on a walk, during a car ride. "Hey, you were working on fractions today — can you explain to me what a common denominator is?" The casual frame makes it safe to say "I don't know" without shame.

**When they say "I don't know" — that is the most valuable moment.** Do not be disappointed. Be glad. You have just found the exact point where understanding ends and memorisation begins. That gap is where the real learning needs to happen.

---

## One more thing

These three questions work for every subject, not just maths. "Why does photosynthesis need sunlight?" "What would happen if the temperature were different?" "Can you teach me what an acid is?"

The principle is the same: if your child can explain *why*, adapt to *change*, and *teach* the concept — they understand it. If they cannot, they have memorised it, and the understanding is still waiting to arrive.

You do not need to be a subject expert to ask these questions. You just need to be curious — and willing to hear "I don't know" without turning it into a problem.

That moment of "I don't know" is not a failure. It is the beginning of learning.
