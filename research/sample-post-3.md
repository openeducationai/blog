Here is a rule you have believed since you were seven years old.

Division makes things smaller.

Check it. 6 ÷ 2 = 3. Smaller. 20 ÷ 4 = 5. Smaller. 100 ÷ 10 = 10. Smaller still. The rule has never let you down.

Now try this one: 6 ÷ ½.

The answer is 12. Bigger than what you started with.

If something in you resisted that, you have just felt what your child feels in class 6, sitting in front of a textbook that has calmly contradicted four years of arithmetic and moved on to the next exercise. They are not being careless. They are noticing something real, and nobody is stopping to explain it.

## The rule was never wrong. It was incomplete.

Your instinct learned "division makes things smaller" from a very particular set of examples: whole numbers, divided by whole numbers, and every divisor was at least 2. In that world the rule is perfectly true.

But the rule quietly carried the boundary of those examples inside it, and nobody wrote the boundary down. The moment a divisor drops below 1, the rule inverts. Not bends. Inverts.

This is the shape of the difficulty. Your child is not failing to learn a new rule. They are being asked to unlearn an old one that has worked flawlessly for four years, and they were never told it had limits.

## "Flip and multiply" is an answer, not a reason

When a child asks why 6 ÷ ½ is bigger, they are usually told this:

> "Flip the second fraction and multiply. 6 × 2 = 12."

That gets the right answer every time. It explains nothing.

A child who asks *why does it get bigger* and receives *because you flip it* has been handed a procedure in place of a reason. They will apply it correctly on Monday, and by Thursday they will not remember which fraction gets flipped, because there is nothing underneath the rule to hold it in place.

Worse, the procedure teaches the child that the question was inappropriate. Ask why, receive a recipe, conclude that mathematics does not answer why. That conclusion is the actual damage, and it lasts far longer than any confusion about fractions.

## The real cause: division has two meanings, and school teaches only one

Here is the part that is almost never said out loud.

Division means two different things, and they are taught as though they are one thing.

**Meaning 1: sharing.** "6 ÷ 2" means *six sweets shared equally between two children. How many does each child get?* Three.

This is how division is introduced in almost every primary classroom in India. It is concrete, it is fair, it involves sweets. It works.

**Meaning 2: fitting.** "6 ÷ 2" means *how many 2s fit inside 6?* Also three.

Both meanings give the same answer for whole numbers, so nobody notices there are two. They travel together for four years, indistinguishable.

Then the divisor becomes ½, and they come apart.

Try the sharing meaning: six sweets shared between *half a child*. The sentence is meaningless. There is no such thing as half a child, and the mental picture the student has relied on since class 2 simply dies.

Now try the fitting meaning: how many halves fit inside 6? Each whole holds two halves. There are six wholes. Twelve halves fit.

Nothing collapsed. The answer is bigger, and it is bigger for an obvious reason: the pieces got smaller, so more of them fit.

**Your child's confusion is not about fractions. It is that the only meaning of division they own is the one that stops working.**

## Once you can read it, you can predict it

The fitting meaning does something the sharing meaning never could. It lets you answer without calculating.

Ask: is the divisor bigger or smaller than 1?

- Divisor **bigger than 1**: each piece is larger than one unit, so fewer than 6 of them fit. The answer comes out smaller. This is the world your instinct grew up in.
- Divisor **exactly 1**: exactly 6 fit. Nothing moves.
- Divisor **smaller than 1**: each piece is less than one unit, so more than 6 fit. The answer comes out bigger.

The cutoff sits at 1, and it has always sat at 1. Primary school just never handed you a divisor on the far side of it.

Try it without computing. Is 20 ÷ 0.5 bigger or smaller than 20? The divisor is below 1, so bigger. Is 20 ÷ 1.5 bigger or smaller? Above 1, so smaller. Is 20 ÷ 0.01 bigger or smaller? Below 1 and tiny, so very much bigger.

A student who has this can check their own homework. That is a different relationship to mathematics than one who is waiting to be told whether they got it right.

## This is not really about fractions

The pattern here outlives the topic.

Your child will meet it again with multiplication, when they discover that ½ × ½ = ¼ and that "multiplying makes things bigger" was also a rule with an invisible boundary. They will meet it again with squaring, when 0.5² turns out to be 0.25. They will meet it in class 11 with limits, and in class 12 with integration.

Every time, the shape is identical: a rule was learned from a restricted set of examples, the restriction was never stated, and the rule breaks the moment the examples widen.

The students who cope are not the ones with better memories. They are the ones who learned, early, that a rule comes with conditions and it is fair to ask what they are. That habit is worth more than any individual rule, and it starts with being taken seriously the first time you say *but why does it get bigger*.

## What you can do tonight

If your child is working on fractions this year, class 6 or 7 usually, try this before you look at any homework.

1. Ask them: you have 3 metres of cloth, and each piece needs a quarter of a metre. How many pieces can the tailor cut? Let them work it out however they like. Many children get 12 without touching a formula.
2. Now ask them to write down the sum that answers that question. They will arrive at 3 ÷ ¼.
3. Ask them why the answer is bigger than 3.

They already know. They worked it out in step 1. The only thing you have done is show them that the arithmetic and the cloth are the same question, which is the connection the textbook left out.

## The one you can see, and the ones you cannot

You have just repaired a gap. It took four minutes, and it worked because somebody told you exactly where to look.

That is the part that does not scale. Your child is carrying other gaps right now, and you do not know which ones. Neither do they. That is what makes them gaps rather than questions: a child who knows what they do not understand can put their hand up. These do not announce themselves. They sit quietly under four years of correct answers, and then surface in class 11 as "she used to be good at maths."

Nobody can watch for all of them. Not a parent at the dining table, not a teacher with sixty students, not the child. The number of places a rule can be learned without its boundary runs into the hundreds across a school career, and every one of them is invisible until it is expensive.
