What is −3 × −2?

You know the answer. It is 6. Positive six.

Now explain *why*.

If you paused — even for a second — you are in good company. Most adults can state the rule ("a negative times a negative is a positive") but cannot explain the reason. They learned the rule, passed the test, and moved on. The explanation never arrived.

Your child is in the same position right now, except they have not yet learned to hide the confusion. They are sitting with a textbook that says −3 × −2 = 6 and a voice in their head saying *but why?*

This post is about that question. Not the rule — the *reason*. And it turns out the reason is not obvious, not trivial, and not something you can wave away with "just memorise it." Mathematicians argued about negative numbers for centuries. Your child's confusion is not a failure of attention. It is the correct response to a genuinely subtle idea.

---

## The problem with "just think of it as debt"

The most common explanation for negative numbers goes like this:

> "Think of negative numbers as debt. If you owe someone ₹300, that is −300. If you earn ₹200, you add 200, and now you owe only ₹100."

This works beautifully for addition. −300 + 200 = −100. The debt metaphor carries it.

But now try multiplication. What is −3 × −2 in the debt metaphor?

"You owe 3 groups of... negative 2 debts?" The metaphor collapses. Debt explains negative addition. It does not explain negative multiplication. And multiplication is exactly where the confusion lives.

Every teacher who has tried the debt metaphor for multiplication has watched the same thing happen: the student nods politely, writes down the rule, and privately remains confused. The metaphor did not fail because the student was not paying attention. It failed because it was the wrong metaphor for multiplication.

## Three mental models, ranked

Here are three ways to think about why −3 × −2 = 6. They are not equally good.

### Model 1: The rule (weakest)

"A negative times a negative is a positive. Memorise it."

This is not a model at all — it is a rule without a reason. It works for passing tests. It does not work for understanding. A student who knows only this rule will get the right answer on Monday and forget which sign to use by Thursday.

### Model 2: The pattern (better)

Write out the multiplication table for −3, starting from −3 × 3 and working down:

| | Result |
|---|---|
| −3 × 3 | = −9 |
| −3 × 2 | = −6 |
| −3 × 1 | = −3 |
| −3 × 0 | = 0 |
| −3 × −1 | = ? |
| −3 × −2 | = ? |

Look at the results column: −9, −6, −3, 0. Each time the multiplier decreases by 1, the result increases by 3. The pattern is clear and undeniable.

So −3 × −1 = 3. And −3 × −2 = 6.

The pattern model is powerful because the student discovers the rule instead of being told it. You do not say "a negative times a negative is positive." You say "continue the pattern and tell me what comes next." The student arrives at the conclusion themselves.

**[Visual suggestion: A number line showing the pattern descending from −9 to 0, then the dotted continuation ascending to 3 and 6. Each step is labelled with the corresponding multiplication. The pattern's rhythm is visible — each jump is +3, and the jumps do not stop at zero.]**

This is the model we use most often in padho.ai's coaching sessions for class 7-8 students. When a student types "why is negative times negative positive?", the AI coach does not state the rule. It builds this table, one row at a time, and asks the student to predict the next entry. Nine times out of ten, the student gets it right — and the smile on their face is the smile of someone who discovered something, not someone who was told something.

### Model 3: The direction model (strongest)

This one requires a small investment of imagination, but it pays off permanently.

Think of multiplication as having two parts: a *size* and a *direction*.

- Positive means "keep going the same direction."
- Negative means "reverse direction."

So:

- 3 × 2 = 6. Start at zero, face right (positive direction), take 3 steps, do it 2 times. You are at 6.
- 3 × −2 = −6. Start at zero, face right, take 3 steps, but *reverse direction* (because of the negative). You are at −6.
- −3 × 2 = −6. Start at zero, face *left* (because −3 is negative), take 3 steps, do it 2 times. You are at −6.
- −3 × −2 = 6. Start at zero, face left, take 3 steps, then *reverse direction*. Reversing left gives you right. You are at 6.

**Reversing a reversal brings you back to the original direction.** That is why a negative times a negative is positive. It is not a rule — it is a consequence of what "negative" means. Negative means "reverse." Two reversals cancel.

Four scenarios play out: (1) walks right to 6, (2) walks right then flips to land at −6, (3) walks left to −6, (4) walks left then flips — ending up at 6. The double-reversal is visually obvious. The reader should be able to replay each scenario by clicking.]**

This model works for all negative number operations, not just this one example. It generalises. And once a student *sees* the double reversal, they never forget it — because it is not a rule stored in memory, it is a picture stored in intuition.

## Why this matters beyond negative numbers

The confusion about negative numbers is not really about negative numbers. It is about what happens when a student is given a rule without a reason.

Rules without reasons are brittle. They work in familiar contexts and break in unfamiliar ones. They are the foundation of the rote-learning pattern that plagues Indian education: memorise the formula, apply it to the standard question, score marks, forget it in two weeks.

A student who understands *why* −3 × −2 = 6 — who has seen the pattern, who can visualise the double reversal — has something stronger than a rule. They have a mental model. And a mental model transfers. It applies to the next confusing concept, and the one after that.

This is the difference between a student who can answer "what is −3 × −2?" and a student who can answer "why is −3 × −2 positive?" The first student memorised. The second student *understands*.

## What you can do tonight

If your child is studying integers this year — class 6 or 7, usually — try this after dinner:

1. Write out the pattern table (Model 2) together. Do not fill in the last two rows. Ask them to predict.
2. If they get it right, ask them *why* the pattern continues. See if they can articulate the reason, even loosely.
3. If they enjoy it, try the direction model (Model 3). Walk across the room together. Face one direction. Now reverse. Now reverse again. Where are you facing?

You are not teaching them negative numbers. You are teaching them that mathematics has *reasons* — and that they are capable of finding them.
